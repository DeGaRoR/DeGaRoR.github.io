Machina Speculatrix

Notes on the model — what is simulated, and what is not

In 1948 W. Grey Walter built two three-wheeled machines, Elmer and Elsie, at the Burden Neurological Institute in Bristol. Each had one photocell, one touch contact, two valves, two motors — and a lamp on its nose that switched off whenever the photocell was driven. Because each machine both emitted and read light, a pair of them formed a closed loop through the world. Walter photographed them in a dark room with the shutter open; the lamp drew its own trace on the plate. This page is that dark room.

Photoreception

Each machine carries two photocells, splayed symmetrically about its heading, each with a finite field of view and a cosine (Lambertian) angular response. Every lit lamp in the arena — machines and placed beacons alike — contributes to both cells:

rᵢⱼ = |xⱼ − xᵢ| a(r) = ( r₀ / (r₀ + r) )^p raw = Σⱼ Iⱼ · a(rᵢⱼ) · max(0, cos φⱼ) · W(φⱼ)

p = 2 is the inverse-square law with the singularity softened at short range; p = 1 is easier to read on a small screen and is the default. W is the field-of-view window. Cells saturate the way real photoresistors do, so a bright lamp at arm's length does not swamp the arithmetic:

s = κ·raw / (1 + κ·raw) s ∈ [0,1)

There is no shadowing: bodies do not occlude one another. That is the one real physical liberty taken here.

Motor wiring

Braitenberg's vehicles 2a and 2b, which is also Walter's circuit. Crossed excitation steers toward light, uncrossed steers away:

toward : v_left = v₀ + g·s_right v_right = v₀ + g·s_left away : v_left = v₀ + g·s_left v_right = v₀ + g·s_right

Differential drive, integrated directly: v = (v_l+v_r)/2, ω = (v_l−v_r)/b. No inertia, no wheel slip.

The dazzle inversion

Walter's tortoise was drawn to moderate light and repelled by bright light — a moth that does not fly into the flame. Above the dazzle threshold the wiring flips from crossed to uncrossed, which is what produces the orbits you see around a strong beacon rather than collisions with it.

The lamp, and why it matters

The lamp is not decoration. It is the machine's only output into the shared medium, and it is anti-correlated with its input: the lamp burns while the photocell is dark, and goes out when the cell is driven. Switching uses a Schmitt trigger (thresholds θ±h) so it cannot chatter, and the filament has a thermal lag τ, so brightness rises and falls rather than stepping.

This single rule is the whole social engine. Two machines approach each other's lamps; approaching extinguishes both lamps; losing the signal they wander; wandering re-lights them; they approach again. Nothing settles. Walter called it a dance, and read it as a crude model of mutual recognition.

Battery and the hutch

With the battery model on, charge drains with speed and with lamp output, and the dazzle threshold scales with charge. A full machine is repelled by bright light. A flat machine is not — so it runs straight at the brightest thing in the room. Mark a beacon as a hutch and that brightest thing is a charger. Walter's tortoises fed themselves this way, and so do these. It is one parameter doing the work of an entire foraging behaviour.

The shell

Walter's shell was a contact switch: any obstacle closed a circuit that overrode the photocell with a push-and-turn oscillation, and the lamp flickered while it ran. Here, wall contact triggers the same reflex — spin away for half a second, lamp stuttering.

Reading the display

  • The cones are the fields of view; their brightness is the live cell reading.
  • The ring around each machine is its charge.
  • Warm bodies are seekers, cold bodies are shy — wired uncrossed, permanently avoidant, until their charge runs low.

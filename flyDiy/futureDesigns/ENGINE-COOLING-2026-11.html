Engine cooling: making the openings mean something

Today the radiators, scoops and inlets are drawn but not read. Cooling costs a fixed drag per kilowatt of heat, whatever is on the cowl. This page measures what the cards draw, proposes one small model, and shows what it would change: the climb on a hot day, the drag, the certificate, and the pusher that has nowhere to take its air.

"The Mustang has this huge protrusion and it has to serve a purpose; I'd rather that be encoded in physics."

In one screen
TodayNothing drawn is read

Cooling drag = a constant × kW of heat. The wing radiators' own comment says "drawn here, billed once". The oil gauge shows 82 °C whenever the engine runs.

Light planesBarely move

Cub, V2-172 and Jodel pass a 38 °C climb with 1.3 to 3.4 × the air they need. Their cooling drag moves by less than 0.005 m², under 1 km/h.

GapCowled pushers can't breathe

Every cowl opening faces the propeller, which on a pusher faces backwards. list:6 Twin busher 4b has 0.004 m² of inlet, facing aft. It needs 0.07.

ErrorBig engines pay 5× too much

The per-kW law charges the Spitfire WIP 0.32 m² of cooling drag. The momentum of the air it actually needs says about 0.06.

2 · The physics, kept simple

Heat in, air through, momentum lost

A pressure cowl as the model sees it. Three measured areas (inlet, outlet, cylinders left in the wind) and two constants per cooling path. No fins, no CFD.

Four lines

ΔT = ε · (T_limit − T_ambient)

The air may warm by ΔT before the engine is too hot. On a hot day the gap is smaller, so it needs more air. Fins: 160 °C and ε 0.30 → 43 K at 15 °C, 37 K at 38 °C. Radiator: 110 °C, ε 0.55. Electric pack: 70 °C, which makes it the most hot-day sensitive.

ṁ_need = Q / (cp · ΔT) cp = 1005 J/kg·K
ṁ_pass = κ · ρ · V_inlet · A_in (capped by the outlet)

κ is the inlet velocity ratio: about 0.45 behind a fixed exit, 0.6 with the exit open wide. V_inlet is the true airspeed plus a share of the slipstream: an actuator-disc estimate gives 1.09 to 1.27 in the probed climbs for a nose inlet; a pusher's forward inlets get nothing. On the ground the slipstream is all there is.

CdA_cool = 2 ṁ (1 − r) / (ρV) = 2 κ A (1 − r)

The drag is the momentum the air loses: in at V, out at r·V (r ≈ 0.7 for a cowl exit, 0.8 for a radiator duct). For fixed geometry it does not depend on speed. The outlet sets the flow, not the inlet: a bigger inlet spills, a small outlet throttles everything.

What the Mustang's scoop is for

The matrix loses pressure with the square of the speed through it, so a big face fed by a diffuser keeps r near 1 and the drag low. The heat added in a duct is a small ramjet (the Meredith effect, about 0.1·M² of the heat): under 1 % of the duct's drag at a light aeroplane's speed, a large share at a fighter's. One line of physics, and the reason the scoop is that big.

  • The cowls fitted to real reference models land at 1.3 to 3.4: the constants put real aeroplanes where real aeroplanes are. That is the calibration target, held by a gate.
  • Cruise margins of 2.6 to 6.4 are air a fixed exit pays for, which is why fast aeroplanes grew cowl flaps.
  • The Spitfire's taxi margin of 0.54 matches its history: Spitfires overheated waiting on the ground.
  • A turbo or supercharged engine keeps its power as the air thins, so it runs hotter as it climbs. A naturally aspirated one cools better high up.

Cooling drag: today against momentum

With the outlet sized for the hot climb (the default for every card that draws none):

today: kCool × kWproposed: 2κA(1−r)

Air-cooled light cards move by under 0.005 m² (under 1 km/h). The liquid pushers were overcharged by about half. The Spitfire's blue bar is cut: 0.32 m² today against 0.06. One constant per kW, tuned on light aeroplanes climbing at 25 to 35 m/s, charges an aeroplane climbing at 70 m/s about five times too much.

4 · The pusher gap

Every opening faces the propeller

On list:6 Twin busher floats 4b the cowl's face aperture nets 0.004 m² round the spinner, and its 0.006 m² scoop mouth faces aft. list:41 Super Petrel LS draws no opening at all. A pusher without a cowl (list:8, list:21) cools in the open and is fine.

The smallest editor addition: two rows on the existing scoop.

  • cw_scoopFace: 0 = faces the propeller (today, byte-identical), 1 = faces the flight. On a tractor both are the same; a pusher born from now gets 1.
  • cw_scoopAz: belly (today's), a cheek pair, or dorsal (a Long-EZ or Cozy style).
  • Later, if wanted: a NACA flush inlet as a scoop style (no protrusion, about 1.5 × the area).

The Twin busher then needs about 0.07 m² of forward-facing mouth (a belly scoop near 30 × 25 cm, or two cheeks), and its aft face becomes the outlet, helped by the propeller's inflow: the way real Rotax pushers and canards do it.

5 · What the player sees

A gauge that means something

Engine temperature follows T_amb + (Q / cp·ṁ) / ε. Amber: "engine hot". Over the red line the engine pulls back power (2 % per K, to 30 %), and the player feels it in the climb. No damage in v1.

Moments it makes

  • A heavy climb out of a summer strip runs hot; lower the nose, gain speed, it cools.
  • A turbo Rotax climbing to 5 000 m runs hot: step-climb.
  • A pusher taxiing on a hot day, waiting at the hold.
  • An electric pack on a hot day: a hard climb gets shorter.
  • The Jodel's big face: lots of margin, more drag if its exit is drawn big.

Cowl flaps: not in v1 (the exit is what is drawn, or sized for you). In v2, one cowl row the pilot opens on the ground and in the climb and closes in cruise, automatic by default. The radiators' existing radFlap row becomes their exit opening.

6 · The certificate

A cooling row, and a clear failure

Two rows in the design check the bench already reads (64b_gen_design.js, tiers in GEN_CERT_TIER). The hot-day climb is a safety row: an engine that overheats in the climb is not safe. The ground row is commercial: impractical, not unsafe.

Existing certificates (R3: a game update is not a change to the aeroplane)

A certificate is a signature on the design's major rows, with no version tag. But the bench re-signs at every roll-out (74_career.js:361), so a new failing row would withdraw a certificate the next time a plane rolled out. The proposal:

  • The rows land proposed: a warning until the user rules, after a fleet sweep.
  • After the ruling, a signature records the rule set it was signed under. An unchanged airframe signed before the cooling rule keeps its pass, with a notice: "Certified before the cooling rule. In a hot climb this engine gets 0.6 of the air it needs: climb at reduced power on hot days, watch the oil temperature." That is an operating limit in R2's sense, not a withdrawal. The live gauge tells the same truth in flight.
  • Any major change (cowl, engine, structure) re-runs the bench, and the row judges like every other.
  • Maker planes must pass before they ship (a gate over the catalogue).
  • Affected today: list:6 Twin busher floats 4b (official) and list:41 Super Petrel LS (internal). Everything else probed passes.

8 · Questions

For you to rule

1. The hot case
The regulation's 38 °C at sea level, full power at Vy, or the game's 1 000 m strip at ISA+20? Default: 38 °C at sea level. The strip stays on the performance page.
2. The tiers
Hot-day climb = safety, ground = commercial. Default: yes, both landing as proposed first.
3. Existing certificates
Kept until the design's major rows change, with a notice, or re-checked at the next roll-out? Default: kept, with the notice (R3).
4. In flight
Live oil temperature and a power pull-back over the red line, or also engine damage after minutes over the limit? Default: no damage in v1.
5. Cowl flaps
Default: v2, a cowl row, automatic.
6. The pusher scoop
"Faces the flight" plus belly / cheeks / dorsal on the existing scoop, NACA flush later? Default: yes, NACA flush later.
7. Undrawn outlets
An undrawn outlet counts as sized for the hot climb (old builds barely move), or as nothing (they fail)? Default: sized for you, with a design aid saying "draw it".
8. Meredith
The one-line thrust credit on drawn radiator ducts? Default: yes. It rounds to zero on everything the garage flies today.

# AIRCRAFT SKIS: reference for modelling and physics (13 Nov 2026)

Scope: skis as a new landing-gear variant, generic across light aircraft, with the Pilatus PC-6 Porter as the first worked example.
Method: text research only (web search + page reads). No files or images downloaded.
Labels used below:
- **[SRC]**: stated by the cited page.
- **[FORUM]**: owner/forum post. Useful, but not authoritative.
- **[SNIPPET]**: seen only in a search-result excerpt. The page itself was not readable (PDF, 403 or 404). Verify before relying on it.
- **[EST]**: my estimate or derivation. Not from a source.

## 0. One-page summary for the team

- **Four main families.** (a) Straight / plain skis replace the wheel and work on snow only. (b) Fixed wheel-penetration skis: the tyre pokes through a slot in the ski. (c) Retractable / hydraulic wheel-skis: the ski is lowered below the tyre, or raised above it. (d) Tail skis and nose skis go with these.
- **Straight-ski rigging is always the same three parts.** A rear limit ("check") cable sets the maximum nose-up angle. A front safety cable limits nose-down droop. A bungee or spring pulls the toe up against the rear cable. Typical flight incidence is +1 to +6 deg nose-up relative to the wing ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot), or 0 to +5 deg per AC 43.13-2B.
- **Ski bottoms.** Bare aluminium on snow has a friction coefficient of about 0.35-0.40. PTFE or polyethylene has about 0.02-0.05. That is why everyone fits UHMW plastic bottoms ([SRC] Engineering Toolbox).
- **Straight skis have no brakes.** The pilot stops with idle power, snow drag and full up-elevator (tail ski dragging). He turns with prop-wash over the rudder in short power bursts. Add-on "ski drags" (carbide picks driven by the brake pedals) exist but are rare.
- **Take-off performance.** Plan at least 10 % more take-off run on good snow. On wet or powder snow it can reach 2-3x the normal distance.
- **Cruise penalty.** About 15 kt on a 300 hp Cessna 185 (130 kt on wheels vs 115 kt on skis at 65 % power).
- **PC-6 Porter.** The factory option is hydraulic wheel-skis on the mains (lowered below the wheels), a tailwheel ski, and reinforced V-struts. Mount Cook (NZ) flies them on the Tasman Glacier. No published ski dimensions were found; estimates are given in section 3.

**Retractable mechanisms by maker.** All are [SRC] Backcountry Pilot unless marked otherwise.
- **Wipaire (formerly Fluidyne, the "AirGlide" line):** a hydraulic ram swings the ski under the tyre, so the tyre rests on top of the ski. It is a non-penetration design: "a full ski OR a wheel" ([SNIPPET] Wipaire Top Cub sheet). An electro-hydraulic power pack adds 17 lb.
- **Federal AWA/AWB (out of production):** a hydraulic ram on a long arm extends the ski below the tyre.
- **Fli-Lite (Federal, out of production):** a U-shaped arm carries the ski, and a hydraulically driven plate moves under the tyre.
- **Airglas / Landes GLH3000 and LH4000:** composite hydraulic retractables. Landes also made a thermoplastic retractable at 39 lb per ski.
- **Genaire Twin Otter wheel-skis:** the aircraft hydraulics drive a "sling" on each ski that draws the ski up under the wheel to make a flat ski surface. The design is "wheel through", so the pilot can choose a ski landing or a wheel landing. The rigging uses cables, links and "shock units" and is described as "self-trimming in flight". Made since 1986. [SRC] https://genaireltd.com/skis/
- **Rosti-Fernandez 8001 (Solo Aviation, Germany):** hydraulic, and called the lightest on the market at the time of writing (Husky).
- **Historic NZ design (Wigley, 1955, Auster):** wheels raised and lowered through slots in wooden skis via pivots, hinges and linkages. Sources disagree: one says a hydraulic lever, another a hand crank operated from the window. https://www.1964.co.nz/?p=20449 , https://www.nbr.co.nz/no-8-re-wired-ski-plane/ . The refined Mount Cook design used "hydraulically operated skis with plastic soles" and was later fitted to the PC-6. https://accidentallywesanderson.com/places/ski-plane/

**Rules of thumb (sizing).**
- The model number is roughly the aircraft gross weight in lb, e.g. "3000" means 3,000 lb ([SRC] Aero table above). Ratings are a weight-bearing rating per ski, "in the same manner as floats", not a surface-area rating ([SNIPPET] AASF Ski Flying Hints).
- **Ground pressure [EST], derived from the table.** Aero M3000H: 72 x 16 = 1,152 sq in per ski, carrying about 1,500 lb, so about 1.3 psi (gross outline area). LW3600: 1,795 sq in carrying about 1,800 lb, so about 1.0 psi. **Design target for light skis: about 1.0-1.5 psi (7-10 kPa) static per main ski.** Compare: a WWII P-38 ski at 640 lb/sq ft (4.4 psi) "sank in deep snow" (https://www.vintagewings.ca/stories/skis-and-floats).
- **Length to width.** Light straight skis run about 4.5:1 to 8:1 (M3000H 4.5:1; M1500 8:1). The trend over time has been shorter and wider: early long, narrow skis were hard to steer and dug in ([SRC] Model Aviation, citing full-size practice: https://library.modelaviation.com/article/skis-snow-operations).
- Some owners extend the UHMW bottom past the ski edges. Reports range from about 1 in to 1.75-2 in per side, and "up to about 10 %" was mentioned as allowable ([FORUM] cessna170.org; https://backcountrypilot.org/forum/help-on-rigging-for-aero-3000-skis-21326?start=20).

### 2.2 Tip, keel, runners and bottom

- **Bottom material.** UHMW polyethylene riveted or bolted to the ski bottom ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot). The forum suggests 3/16-1/4 in thickness. One measured Federal A-1500 had a .120 in aluminium skin plus .125 in plastic ([FORUM] https://backcountrypilot.org/forum/plastic-on-federal-1500-s-18372).
- **Older and other bottoms.** Older skis had steel wear strips (runners), and steel strips "WILL get you stuck if parked on snow"; the advice is to replace them with UHMW ([SRC] https://backcountrypilot.org/knowledge-base/pilots/91-knowledge-base/pilots/164-intro-to-ski-flying-operations). Other bottoms: wood (early, Atlee Dodge), aluminium (Federal, Fluidyne, Datum), fibreglass (Landes since the 1950s), thermoplastic, carbon (Summit, Solo) ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot). "Polyethylene sheathing" is cut and shattered by cold and impacts ([SRC] FAA text).
- **Tip upturn.** No full-size number was found. [EST] From typical photos, the upturn rises about 4-8 in over the last 12-18 in of a light ski. **Measure this from photos.** The model-scale rule is "bend the front up about 1/2 in on a 7.5 in ski" (about 7 % of length), with a slight upturn at the heel too, so the ski does not dig in when pushed backwards ([SRC] Model Aviation).
- **Keel / skeg.** Pilots note "thin sharp blades suit hard-pack and ice; wide long skis suit powder" ([SRC] FAA text). No keel dimensions were found. Many skis show one or two shallow longitudinal runners or wear strips (see the photo list).
- **Ski camber.** Skis have camber that "relaxes" under load ([FORUM] Aero 3000 thread).

### 2.3 Pedestal, pivot and axle position

- **Pedestal.** A box or plate structure on the top centre of the ski. It carries a bushing for the gear axle, and often a second 1 in bushing "about half way up" so a ski dolly can be fitted ([FORUM] https://forum.cessna170.org/forums/viewtopic.php?f=5&t=3016&start=30). The ski butts against the axle collar ([FORUM] Aero 3000 thread).
- **Pedestal height limit (AC 43.13-2B).** The pedestal must not exceed **130 % of the axle centreline height with wheel and tyre installed**. This limits moment loads on the gear ([SNIPPET] quoting AC 43.13-2B ch. 5; https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/ac%2043.13-2b.pdf).
- **Pivot fore/aft position.**
  - Full-size practice moved the axle toward the heel. Norseman skis had the axle about **1/3 of ski length from the rear**; J-3 and Taylorcraft about **25 % from the rear** ([SRC] Model Aviation, describing full-size practice; secondary).
  - A homebuilt note says 30-40 % of length aft of the nose ([SNIPPET], conflicts with the above).
  - **[EST] For modelling: put the pivot 55-70 % of length back from the tip,** and calibrate against photos.

### 2.4 Rigging: check cables, bungee or spring, safety cable

Straight skis and most penetration skis use this arrangement ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot):
1. **Aft limit cable (the "check cable").** Runs from the rear of the ski to a fuselage point aft of the main gear attachment; rear float fittings are often used. Its length **sets the ski's maximum nose-up angle in flight**.
2. **Forward cable (the "safety cable").** Runs from the ski toe to a point near the firewall or the forward gear attachment. It **stops the toe from dropping** (ski "tucking") if the bungee fails.
3. **Bungee (shock cord) or steel spring.** Attaches at the ski front, at the same point as the forward cable. It takes up slack and **keeps the ski pulled toe-up against the aft cable**.
4. **"Crud cutter" / crust-cutting cable.** A short lead cable ahead of the bungee at the ski toe. It protects the bungee from snow crust ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot photo caption).

**Worked example of the bungee moment [EST].** For a PC-6 (W = 6,173 lb, Vne about 151 kt = 174 mph), 0.36e-5 x 6173 x 174^2 gives about **670 ft-lb ultimate**. This is the moment the elastic trim must resist.

**Spring vs bungee.** Bungees deteriorate fast under tension, so they are unhooked at the lower end overnight ([SRC] FAA text). Re-hooking them needs two people. The Alaska Airmen recommend a spring at the front, because a drooped ski "even if caught by the check cable can be a very serious event" ([SNIPPET] AASF). Atlee Dodge springs exist but are not approved on every ski ([FORUM]).

**Retractable wheel-skis replace the cables.** They use hydraulic riggers or linkages, and are "self-trimming" ([SRC] Genaire). Some keep check cables or bungees as well ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot). The weight and balance must be computed for both wheels-down and skis-down, "since the arm changes substantially" ([FORUM] https://www.supercub.org/forum/threads/faa-stc-retractable-wheel-ski-options.54070/). Wipline 3600 arms are about **10 in centre-to-centre** between axles ([FORUM] same source).

**Tail ski mount (patent US4390151).** Two pedestals straddle the tailwheel fork, and an extended tailwheel axle runs through both. A slot lets the wheel protrude. An attitude arm clamped to the fork carries a **safety cable to the toe** (nose-drop stop) and a **spring to the heel**, which pulls the toe up against that stop. The fork keeps full caster and steering. https://patents.google.com/patent/US4390151

### 3.4 What the PC-6 assembly looks like (description to confirm against photos)

This is built from the found facts (hydraulic, lowerable, main wheel-skis, tail ski with strut housing, reinforced V-struts) plus the general wheel-ski pattern. **Every geometric detail here is [EST] until confirmed by the photos in section 5.**

- **Side view, main gear.** The PC-6 main leg is a long, forward-braced V-strut with an oleo shock strut to the fuselage, and the wheel sits at the bottom. The wheel-ski is a long, flat ski with an upturned toe, carried under and around the wheel. The tyre passes through a slot in the ski, the same "wheel-through" pattern as Wigley and Genaire.
  - In **ski mode**, a hydraulic jack (or a linkage driven by one) pushes the ski down below the tyre. The aircraft then rests on the ski, and the tyre sits clear above the snow.
  - In **wheel mode**, the ski is pulled up, so the tyre protrudes below the ski bottom and touches the runway.
  - Expect a hydraulic line running down the leg, a ski pedestal or arm pivoting on or near the axle, and fore/aft limit links or cables to keep the ski trimmed in flight.
- **Front view.** The ski is about as wide as or wider than the tyre (estimate 0.55-0.7 m vs a tyre of about 0.25-0.3 m), centred under the wheel, with the slot visible. The reinforced V-struts look like the standard ones, but beefier.
- **From below.** A plastic sole (Mount Cook design) runs nearly the full length, with a slot for the tyre roughly mid-length (a little aft of centre). There may be runners or keel strips along the edges.
- **Tail.** A small ski on the tailwheel, with the tailwheel poking through it. The "strut housing" suggests the tail ski attaches to a modified tailwheel strut.
- **Cockpit.** A pump (hydraulic hand pump or electric) and a selector with position indication. No PC-6 cockpit description was found.

**Notes for the physics programmer**
- **Static breakout.** Static friction exceeds kinetic friction, typically about 2x on snow.
- **Freeze-in.** Warm skis melt the surface and refreeze, bonding the ski to the snow; "no amount of engine power" moves a frozen-in ski ([SNIPPET] FAA text). Model this as a bond that grows with parked time when (ski temperature > 0 °C at stop) or (on overflow/slush). Freeing methods: rocking the wings or struts, swinging the tail, a rudder/power burst ([SRC] FAA text; AvWeb).
- **Pilot cooling routine.** After landing: stop, wait, taxi one ski length, wait, then shut down ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot Part 2).
- **Very cold snow** loses the meltwater film, so ski performance "will suffer significantly" (Backcountry Pilot Part 2).
- **Deep snow.** Ploughing and compaction drag dominates over sliding friction. [EST] Model it as resistance proportional to ski frontal area times sinkage depth, plus extra drag from a protruding wheel on penetration skis.

### 4.2 No brakes: stopping and turning

- **Straight skis have no wheel brakes and no parking brake** ([SRC] FAA text; Backcountry Pilot Part 2). Wheel-skis in wheel mode keep their brakes.
- **Stopping.** Idle power plus snow drag. Full up-elevator during the slide-out with a tail ski "will slow the airplane down relatively effectively"; without a tail ski, pulling hard back shortens the stop ([SRC] AvWeb). On glare ice, expect a much longer landing roll and a possible ground loop ([SRC] FAA text).
- **Ski brake / drag (rare add-on).** The Grizzly Claw ski drag is a lever arm on top of the ski near the pedestal. A brake-pedal slave cylinder drives a two-tip carbide pick down through a 2-3 sq in hole in the ski. It is STC'd on aircraft approved for Airglas L2000A/L2500A/L3000 straight skis. https://mcfarlanealaska.com/products/grizzly-claw-ski-drag . Patent equivalent: a spring-returned L-shaped brake plate with a carbide wear plate, slave cylinder on the brake line, https://patents.google.com/patent/US4061296A/en
- **Turning.** A short power blast with full rudder (and nose-down elevator to lighten the tail) swings the aircraft; holding power too long just accelerates it straight ahead. Sideways drift in turns is normal. Turning too sharply digs in a ski, with a ground-loop or nose-over risk, so use wide arcs ([SRC] FAA text; Backcountry Pilot Part 2). Turns load the outside ski, and many stuck incidents happen in turns. Tail-ski side loads in deep snow can bend the aft fuselage ([SRC] AvWeb).
- **Taxi.** Little braking or turning control, and side-skidding, so more room is needed. Skiplanes weathervane and drift in crosswind. On powder, keep enough power to stay on top of the snow ("step-taxi" like a floatplane). On ice or crust, keep power low ([SRC] FAA text). Too much nose-down elevator digs the ski noses in and creates large drag ([SRC] Backcountry Pilot Part 2).
- **Before start.** Tie down or chock, or build snow mounds in front of each ski ([SRC] FAA text).

### 4.4 Glacier, altiport and slope operations

- **Land uphill. Do not stop while climbing the slope; turn crosswise to the slope before stopping, or the aircraft slides back. Take off downhill.** Add power just before touchdown to soften the arrival. Flare to match the slope ([SRC] FAA text; summaries of https://www.flightgear.org/blog/mountain-flying-in-the-french-alps/ and https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2018/july/pilot/landing-uphill-on-ice).
- **Snow assessment.** Fly downhill just above stall with the skis touching the snow. Avoid valley bottoms (icefalls) and check avalanche risk. No flying with continuous cloud cover in Alpine glacier operations, because of flat light and whiteout ([SRC] FAA text; Backcountry Pilot Part 2).
- **Air Alpes altiports.** About 200-300 m long, with slopes up to 36 % ([SRC] de.wikipedia PC-6). TC AC 500-010 requires the ski to accommodate 7.5 deg slopes on the ground.
- **Parking on slopes.** Perpendicular to the incline, skis blocked. Select the uphill fuel tank ([SRC] FAA text).

### 4.5 Cruise drag and weight

- **Cessna 185 (300 hp, 65 % power):** 130 kt on wheels, **115 kt on skis**, 110 kt on floats. https://aviationconsumer.com/uncategorized/cessna-185/
- **Owner reports, C185:** 120 kt on penetration skis vs 135 kt on 8.50 tyres; about 5 kt lost on a modified 185. **Super Cub:** about 172 mph on wheels vs about 155 mph on wheel-skis, but another owner reports only about 3 mph ([FORUM], via search summaries of supercub.org and backcountrypilot.org threads).
- **[EST] Rule for the sim:**
  - Straight skis: about 5-8 % cruise-speed loss.
  - Wheel-skis and penetration skis: about 8-12 % loss.
  - As equivalent flat-plate drag area: about 0.15-0.3 m² per main ski at Porter scale.
  - A broken check cable gives a hanging ski with very large drag ([SRC] FAA text).
- **Weights.** See section 2.1. A light straight ski is about 7-30 lb each. Hydraulic wheel-skis run 84-136 lb per pair on Cub to C180 class, plus a 17 lb power pack.
- **CG.** Retractable skis move the CG. Wipline C2200 owners say the "CG moves forward when retracted" ([FORUM] supercub.org).

### 4.6 Suggested sim model [EST]

These are design suggestions, not sourced.
- **Ski contact.** Model each ski as a rigid plank with three contact probes (toe, pedestal, heel). The upturned toe has no contact until sinkage.
- **Ski pitch.** One pitch DOF about the axle, with a hard stop at +incidence (aft check cable, about +3 to +5 deg), a hard stop at -incidence (safety cable, about -15 to -20 deg), and a bungee/spring torque toward the + stop. On the ground the ski follows the surface within the ±7.5 deg slope range.
- **Friction.** Mu by snow class:

  | Snow | Kinetic mu | Static breakout |
  |---|---|---|
  | cold dry, -10 °C or colder | 0.10-0.20 | about 2x kinetic |
  | ideal, -3 °C | 0.03-0.05 | about 2x kinetic |
  | wet, about 0 °C | 0.08-0.12 | about 2x kinetic |
  | glare ice | 0.02 | about 2x kinetic |

  Add a sinkage term from ground pressure vs snow strength (powder vs packed), with plough drag proportional to sinkage times ski width.
- **Freeze-in.** A bond timer that starts if parked within about 1 min of a fast taxi or landing (warm ski). Freeing it takes rocking or power bursts.
- **No brakes on straight skis.** The brake input does nothing, or drives an optional ski-drag pick (about 0.3-0.5 extra mu on that ski). Rudder authority comes from prop-wash only at low speed. A tail ski gives a drag/caster yaw damping term.
- **Wheel-ski mode switch** on the PC-6, with a hydraulic cycle time. [EST] About 10-20 s; no source found.

Aero Body Plan

The physics should fly the body the player sees. Today three official aeroplanes fly a body that is not drawn: two rod booms fly a full fuselage cone, and the Twin flies a 1.07 m cone behind its back cowl. This page shows each one drawn against flown, the model that replaces it, and what moves.

The rod in twist, checked before touching the structure

You asked to anticipate rigidity and twist under a more accurate model. A node rig on GATE FLEX's own tail-arm method: everything ahead of the drawn pod pinned on every substep, ±150 N up and down at the stab tips. Each cell is the rod's own twist at its last ring, then the stab's roll.

  • The tube element carries the twist. With it off, every rod plane reads 42–45° of stab roll and members tear, at 200 substeps. It must stay in any rod model.
  • Moving the nodes onto the drawn tube is safe for twist with the element on: the rod's own twist stays under 1.1° (2.6° on the Chinook), the stab roll moves −45 % to +16 %, and substeps rise 6–26 %. On the v8 pusher the move goes wrong: its core cabin box runs 0.5 m past the drawn pod, the lattice pinches there, and a member hits 4.2 % strain. The box has to follow the drawing first. This change keeps the nodes where they are (your call 4), so twist is untouched.
  • Strength was overstated. The damage model took a tube's torque limit from the material's ultimate shear. A rod of d/t 75–95 buckles first: the Chinook's 89 × 1.2 mm aluminium rod at 90 MPa, about 1 350 N·m, roughly half the limit the damage model used. The rod's limits now take thin-tube torsional buckling (Timoshenko & Gere) and the shell's bending buckling (0.3 E t/r).
  • Already soft today: most of the Chinook's 15° stab roll is the stab rolling on its post mounting, not the rod (the rod itself turns 1.8°). That mounting is the tail's own matter, flagged.

The model, in five rules

  1. The body is the drawing. The join measures the drawn skin (the gear layer's own sampler of the cage) and the cowl layer (PROPER-COWLS' mesh, cut column by column) into one table, nose to closure. The core reads that table for drag and side area. The structure lattice keeps its own stations.
  2. A body that ends in a base pays for the base. Where the closure is steeper than 20° the flow leaves the skin. The body ahead of that point is a forebody: friction × (1 + 1.5/f1.5) (Hoerner, Fluid-Dynamic Drag ch. 6). The section there is a base at Cd 0.14 (Raymer, Aircraft Design eq. 12.37). A body that closes smoothly (every conventional fuselage) keeps today's rule, Raymer's 1 + 60/f³ + f/400.
  3. Rods and booms are tubes. Friction on their drawn diameter and length (0.004 × π d L, the twin booms' own coefficient), and their side area d × L.
  4. Side force where the side area is. On a pod-and-rod the aft side force acts at the centroid of the drawn aft side area. The twin booms get their own point at the booms' centroid. Conventional bodies keep today's point, which the Cub calibration was fitted on.
  5. No floor on boom layouts. The conventional tail-arm floor (60_gen_spec.js:6359) is skipped on pod-and-rod and twin-boom builds. The Twin's lattice then ends at its drawn pod (−7.5 kg of phantom cone).

Case 1 · Rods

For each rod plane, both layers are checked. The structure is mostly right: the rod flies on the tube element (G314) at the drawn tube's EI and GJ. But its lattice still sits on the cone's stations and bills the cone's bay faces, so it carries 3–9 kg the drawing does not have. The aero body is wrong: it is the phantom cone, with 3 to 7 times the drawn aft side area, acting 0.7–1.4 m further aft.

Which drag law

The drawn geometry is the same in every column; only the drag law differs. Raymer's form factor (model A, today's rule applied to the drawn length) is a fit for slender fuselages. Below a fineness of about 3 it explodes: it charges the v8 pusher's blunt pod Cd 0.83 on its frontal area, close to a flat plate's. Hoerner's forebody-plus-base method is the textbook treatment for a body ending in a base, which is what every pod here is. Body CdA m² · cruise km/h:

GATE AEROBODY

  • Length: for every official plane the flown body runs from the drawn nose to the drawn closure within 2 %. Measured off the headless editor through the load door, the same way the plane loads.
  • Base accounted: a body with a base reports the base's area and drag row (the plaque's drag page), and a closed body reports zero.
  • Rods real: on every rod build, the flown aft section is at most 1.6 × the drawn rod's diameter at mid-rod, and the rod's drag and side rows use its drawn diameter.
  • No floor on booms: no twin-boom or rod build's lattice extends past its drawn pod.

Found on the way

  • Phantom nose cowls. The core derives a cowl for every build (60_gen_spec 4c) and wets it. Rufus has a bare engine yet flies a 0.88 m cowl ahead of its firewall. The pod builds fly the core's cowl instead of their drawn aero nose (Twin −0.59 m flown vs −0.71 drawn). The measured table fixes this for drag; the 4c cowl stays for the engine-enclosure check.
  • The v8 pusher's and v7 twin's core cabin box is 0.4–0.5 m longer than drawn (box 1.70 m, drawn pod 1.16 m). The aero body follows the drawing. The lattice is the frame's question, flagged to A0.
  • Super Petrel (validated reference, not official): its flown nose is 0.43 m short of its drawn hull bow (−7.7 % length). It is outside this gate's list; the measured table covers it when it joins.
  • Float hulls carry no air drag (SEAPLANES-LAND's backlog). Not taken here.

Not now: aft aero pods, the Archaeopteryx

No official plane carries an aft aero pod. The model covers one with no extra code: its rings are in the drawn skin, so the table measures it, and the 20° rule decides whether it closes cleanly (no base) or not. When the "engine not at the back" restriction lifts (the Archaeopteryx branch's aeroAftOver), a pod drawn over a pusher is measured the same way. The pusher-suction question below then applies to it as well.

Your calls (ruled 12 Nov: all defaults)

1. The drag law for bodies that end in a base.Default: B, forebody + base at Cd 0.14. A (Raymer on the drawn length) costs the pods 8–26 km/h of cruise; B-H is a little cleaner than B.
2. A pusher right behind a base: credit its suction?Default: no credit and no debit. A pusher's inflow can reattach a steep afterbody, but the same prop then works in the body's wake. I found no source I could check from the cloud that sizes the net, so it stays out until one is named.
3. The rod's cross-flow coefficient.Default: 0.31, the body row the twin booms already wear. A round tube in cross-flow is nearer 1.2 (Hoerner); that variant is in each rod table (Cn_β 4–6 % higher than with 0.31).
4. The rod's structure.Default: bill the rod as the drawn tube (mass) and no covering aft of the pod, but leave the lattice nodes where they are as the tube element's carriers. Inertia moves 1–3 %. Moving the nodes onto the tube would shorten the solver's step, which is a frame chantier.
5. The conventional six: drawn nose and tail cap too?Default: yes, for WYSIWYG and so the gate holds at 2 %. It moves them by 0.1 km/h or less, and cdWet is not re-solved.
6. The separation angle.Default: 20° (Hoerner's boattail range is 15–20°). The rod pods do not move with it (their closures are steeper than 30°). The Twin's back cowl does: its body reads 0.209 m² at 15°, 0.181 at 20° and 0.136 at 30°.
